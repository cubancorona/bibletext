# What we add, and what we drop

An accounting of the text against the standard in docs/SCRIPTURE_WORKLIST.md:
nothing dropped without an explicit decision, nothing added at all. It exists
so that each item can be decided rather than defended, and it is the input to
the worklist, not a substitute for it.

Two cautions about the figures. Counts drawn from the raw captures, the decoded
caches and a full NKJV download are measured; the rest are marked "not
measured" and should be treated as unknown rather than small. And a handful of
the sharpest claims were re-checked directly against the code: that the Share
verb cleaned a selection while the AI path did not, that the website carried no
copy-cleaning at all, and that verse numbers are inside the selectable text.
All three held when this was written, and the first two were acted on: the AI
path is cleaned by the same helper, and the website emits nothing that needs
cleaning. The third still holds, and is why the first two mattered.

# Accounting: what BibleText adds and drops against its own publishers

Standard applied throughout: nothing is dropped unless explicitly agreed, and nothing is added at all. Four editions are in scope — WEB, WEB Catholic, BSB (decoded from helloao) and NKJV (decoded from API.Bible) — plus the generated red-letter tables, the ten downstream pipelines (search, speech, share text/image, copy verbs, shared links, AI, cross-references, verse-of-day, the website), and the five rendering surfaces. Counts are drawn from direct measurement against the decoded canons and cached captures wherever the source material states this; where a pass could not establish a count, it is reported below as "not measured" rather than estimated.

## 1. Verdict

This is a snapshot of one moment, and the two findings it led with have since been answered. Both are kept as they were written, with the answer beside them, because the reasoning is what made the case for changing them.

Measured, reader-facing drops were substantial and mostly concentrated in a small number of root causes: the BSB lost all 3,091 of its publisher's chapter headings (72,690 characters) outright, with no side-channel to recover them from; the NKJV dropped an estimated but unmeasured ~3,300 section headings and cross-reference lines (including some, mr/sr/r, treated worse than an ordinary footnote); the WEB and WEB Catholic lose nearly all stanza structure in Psalms, Song of Solomon and Wisdom; and Psalm superscriptions, though retained internally, fail to reach seven of the app's own surfaces (search, speech, sharing, the dedicated chapter-copy verb, the website, cross-references, and nine of every 371 verse-of-day rotations). The single worst drop was the BSB's chapter headings, because unlike every other omission audited here, it was not merely unshown — the text never entered the data model at all, so there was nothing left to change one's mind about later. **That drop is closed.** Every edition's headings are decoded into the data model and drawn on every surface, the NKJV's included; and in the NKJV, which is the only feed that puts a note inside a heading block, that note is kept on the heading instead of vanishing with it. The WEB and WEB Catholic stanza gap and the Psalm-title gap stand as written.

On the addition side, every one of roughly 31,000 verses per edition carried a manufactured verse-number-and-joiner character pair, and that pair — along with a literal paragraph-indent character on Apple platforms and the reader's own uncleaned selection — escaped uncleaned into native OS copy/paste on iOS, macOS and Android, into every browser copy from the public website (which had no cleaning code at all), into the Windows/Linux desktop's own Copy command, and into what was transmitted to the external AI provider. The single worst addition was this unclamped, largely undocumented set of escape channels, because it was the one place where app-manufactured characters left the app disguised as the publisher's own words, with no recorded decision anywhere that this should happen. **Those channels are closed**, by two moves rather than one: a shared outbound cleaner (`outbound_text.go`), which the two verbs that were carrying the raw selection — the Windows/Linux desktop pane's Copy and the AI pipeline — now pass through, and — for the system's own Copy, which reads the text storage directly and cannot be intercepted — the removal of the characters themselves, so there is nothing left in the storage to take. Share was already clean and still cleans with its own older helpers rather than with this one.

**The escape-channel additions (rows 1–3).** Every reading surface drew the verse number and a joining space or non-breaking space directly into the same text stream the reader can select. Three of the four ways that selection could leave the app — the operating system's own Copy/Select-All on iOS, macOS and Android; the Windows/Linux desktop pane's own Copy command; and the public website, which had no cleaning code whatsoever — carried that manufactured content through unfiltered, and a fourth, the "Study with AI" pipeline, sent the identical uncleaned selection to an external service, with poem-line structure additionally corrupted into literal `\n` text. Only the app's own Share verb was cleaned. None of it was discussed anywhere as a decision; the code's own rationale for leaving system Copy unclamped addressed a different problem entirely (stopping the footnote apparatus from being misattributed as Scripture), not this one.

All four are closed, and how they divide is the part of this audit worth keeping. Where the app owns the verb, cleaning it is enough, and the two verbs that were leaking — the desktop pane's Copy and the AI pipeline — now go through one shared cleaner (`outboundText`), which is what generalising the Share verb's own helpers was always going to look like. Share and the link it builds are not on that path: they still clean with their own helpers (`plainSelection`, `collapseSpaces`, `stripVerseMarkers`), which take out the verse number and collapse the manufactured spaces but do not map back the small capitals the NKJV's divine name is now drawn in — so a share of one of those verses carries the small-capital form, and, since the cleaned selection is located by matching it inside `Verse.Text`, it is not found there and the share falls back to the legacy citation path. Where it does not own the verb, no cleaner can be reached: the system's Copy reads the text storage directly, and a browser copies the DOM. Those two had to be answered by not manufacturing the character at all, so the HTML panes and the website now join the number to its verse with an ordinary space and set the superscript with markup over an ordinary digit. Only the Fyne-drawn panes, which have no markup to set it with, still write a superscript character — and the shipping one of those is the desktop pane, whose Copy is a verb the app owns.

**The reporter-layout indent.** Because the HTML importer on Apple's reading pane drops CSS `text-indent`, the app substituted two literal Unicode space characters at the front of every paragraph-opening prose verse. A code comment admitted this "carried" into copied text on Apple platforms; neither audit document mentioned it at all. It is the one channel that could not be answered by stripping, since the leak was into the system's own Copy, and it was answered by indenting some other way instead: a first-line head indent on the imported paragraph style on the Apple panes, and on Android a private-use marker rune the bridge deletes as it imports, putting a real leading-margin span in its place.

**The NKJV uppercasing.** Divine-name and small-caps conventions were collapsed into one identical uppercase treatment, and the four verses where Jesus is named ("Matthew 1:21," etc.) had been reviewed and accepted on that basis. Thirty further verses carrying the same mechanism — including Exodus 3:14 and John 8:58's paired "I AM," and a full multi-word phrase in Deuteronomy 28:58 — were never surfaced for the same review, and it was the count of them that made the case. The case change is gone: the spans are kept as offsets, the stored text is the publisher's own casing, and the small capitals are drawn. What the review would have covered is therefore no longer a question about the text — but the two conventions are still stored under one flag, so the typographic distinction between `sc` and `nd` remains lost, as item 8 says.

**Psalm titles.** The text is stored and displayed on the reading pane, yet it independently fails to reach search, speech, sharing, the dedicated chapter-copy command, the website, cross-references, and one in forty-one verse-of-day rotations. No single decision produced this pattern — each surface simply never called the existing accessor.

**BSB headings and NKJV headings/cross-references.** These were the two places where the publisher's own structural apparatus for guiding a reader through a chapter was removed completely, in every instance, with no toggle and, for the BSB, no possible way to recover the text later because it was never captured. Both are captured and drawn now, on one shared model that decides a chapter's order as heading-then-paragraph and hands it to every surface. Two things the recommendation asked for did not come with it: there is still no toggle, so a reader cannot turn headings off, and there is no length guard, so the single 510-character WEB Catholic heading in Greek Daniel 3 — a title fused to a translator's note — is drawn whole.

**WEB/WEB Catholic stanza breaks.** For the books most defined by poetic structure — Psalms, Song of Solomon, and especially Wisdom, whose generated table is empty — the paragraphing a print WEB edition uses is not delivered at all.

## 5. Never agreed

The list that matters most. Each of these is either flagged as an open question with no resolution recorded, or not discussed at all, or the recorded claim about it does not match what the code actually does.

1. **WEB/WEB Catholic stanza-break consequence.** The generator's exclusion of `\b` is a stated rule; its actual, near-total effect on Psalms, Song of Solomon, and Wisdom was never quantified or put to a decision.
2. **NKJV section headings and cross-reference lines.** SETTLED as a drop: they are captured with the publisher's own style name and drawn, and the mr/sr/r cross-reference sub-case — erased with less protection than an ordinary footnote, and never separately named — goes with them, its notes kept on the heading. What is NOT settled is the second half of this item. The style list still matches by exact string, so a related but unlisted style (s5, ms4, mt*, sd*) would fall through to the prose path and into verse text, on the addition side; it has still never been checked against a live feed.
3. **Notes inside NKJV's heading/reference blocks.** No longer dropped — they are kept on the heading that carried them, which is what put them back on the same footing as every other note. The quantity is still unknown: the probe was recommended and never run.
4. **Text or a note before an NKJV chapter's first verse marker.** Unknown quantity, never probed; the existing dismissal ("nothing to key it to") is asserted, not evidenced.
5. **NKJV "Selah."** Flattened exactly as WEB/BSB are (an accepted pattern for those two), but never named for NKJV specifically — 74 verses.
6. **NKJV bold ("bd") character style.** Flattening is asserted as policy, but whether the NKJV feed ever actually contains a "bd" span has never been verified either way.
7. **NKJV wire attributes never read** (Strong's numbers, altnumber/pubnumber print-verse-number variants, and others). No raw capture exists locally to check whether these are ever present.
8. **NKJV uppercasing beyond the four reviewed verses.** Roughly 30 further verses (Exodus 3:14 and John 8:58's "I AM," three "YAH" verses, two "BRANCH" verses, the phrase in Deuteronomy 28:58, Daniel 5's handwriting, the Gospel cross-inscriptions) were never mentioned. The uppercasing itself is gone, so none of them is a question about the stored text any more. Of the 48 runes, twenty-eight are a closing double quotation mark and eighteen — over a third — are a question mark, with the last two the closing bracket and full stop of Mark 7:34's `opened!”).`

11. **Verse-of-day never shows a Psalm's title** (9 of 371 rotation entries). Not discussed anywhere.
12. **Verse-of-day never applies red letter**, despite the rotation being deliberately curated to spotlight Christ (62 of 371 entries, about one day in six). The generic gap is flagged OPEN; this specific figure has never been surfaced.

24. **A latent duplicate-orphan-footnote risk at API.Bible chunk-overlap boundaries.** Verses are deduplicated at these boundaries; the accompanying orphan-note map is not. Zero instances today only because the NKJV happens to have no omitted verses. Not discussed anywhere.
25. **`normalizeVerseSpaces` (NKJV) collapses any run of Unicode whitespace, including a non-breaking space, to one plain ASCII character**, with no way to tell afterward whether a collapsed run was a decode artefact or the publisher's own deliberate non-breaking space. Not discussed anywhere.

**Where passes disagree, direct evidence is preferred:** the styled-pane token-overpaint documentation (item 9) claims an exhaustive 2-verse case where recomputation against the real tables found 48 across all four editions, and finds 34 in the three that can be recomputed here without a licensed cache; the claim that red-letter's secondary-surface gap was "analysed and answered" (item 10) is contradicted by a direct grep across every surface named, which found no such handling anywhere; and the share.go comment describing paragraph-versus-poem-line handling for share-as-image (item 16) is contradicted by tracing the actual call order in the code, which shows both the text and image paths receive the identical restored string before the real defect (a dropped blank segment) occurs later.
